[ABSTRACT]: Wave PDE–ODE cascades model control systems in which
the input is not directly applied to the finite-dimensional plant, but is transmit-
ted through a distributed actuator with elasticity, inertia, and finite propagation
speed. Such models appear in flexible mechanical structures, vibration sys-
tems, transmission cables, acoustic channels, and other engineering systems
where the actuator dynamics cannot be accurately represented by a lumped
input. Due to their ability to capture spatial propagation and energy storage,
wave PDE–ODE models provide a more realistic description than pure input-
delay models. However, the boundary stabilization of these systems remains
challenging. The wave equation is conservative in nature and lacks the natural
dissipative mechanism of parabolic PDEs. In addition, distributed coupling be-
tween the wave displacement, the wave velocity, and the ODE state makes the
closed-loop system structurally more complicated than standard strict-feedback
cascades. Existing predictor and backstepping methods can compensate for dis-
tributed actuator dynamics, but the resulting controllers are often algebraically
involved and may require full knowledge of the distributed PDE state. To ad-
dress these issues, this thesis studies the boundary stabilization problem for a
class of linear ODE systems with wave PDE input dynamics and distributed
coupling effects, and develops a low-gain boundary feedback approach to ob-
tain exponential stability while keeping the controller structurally simple.
     The main contributions of this thesis can be summarized as follows. (1)
The boundary stabilization problem of a wave PDE–ODE cascade system with
distributed displacement and velocity coupling effects is studied. A low-gain
boundary feedback law is proposed, consisting of a low-gain term for the finite-

                                         I
dimensional ODE subsystem and a boundary velocity damping term for the
wave actuator. Unlike backstepping-based controllers, the proposed controller
has a simpler online structure, as it requires only the ODE state and the local
boundary velocity of the wave equation, while the distributed coupling effects
are incorporated through an offline kernel boundary-value problem. This pro-
vides a simpler stabilization scheme for wave PDE–ODE cascade systems, al-
beit with additional spectral conditions on the open-loop ODE subsystem. (2)
The exponential stability of the closed-loop system is rigorously established.
Exploiting the positive Robin elastic support at the uncontrolled boundary and
the controller-injected damping at the controlled boundary, a Lyapunov func-
tional is constructed by combining the low-gain ODE energy, the wave energy,
the endpoint elastic energy, and cross terms for the distributed coupling ef-
fects. Under appropriate low-gain conditions, the closed-loop PDE–ODE cas-
cade system is proved exponentially stable. Numerical simulations further ver-
ify that the proposed controller stabilizes both the finite-dimensional plant state
and the distributed wave state.




[Key words]: PDE–ODE cascade; Wave equation; Boundary stabilization;
Low-gain compensation

1.1    Research Background
      In many control systems, the controller command reaches the ODE plant through a non-

ideal actuator rather than an ideal lumped input. It may first pass through actuator dynamics

associated with a non-negligible transmission length, finite propagation speed, elasticity, in-

ertia, or acoustic storage. In such cases, the actuator is itself a dynamical subsystem: it

stores energy, reflects disturbances at its boundaries, and delivers the commanded action to

the plant only through the evolution of a distributed state. This viewpoint is closely related to

the classical compensation of input delays [1]-[4], but it keeps more of the physical actuator

dynamics than a pure delay representation.

      Wave equations provide a natural model for actuator dynamics in which propagation

and energy storage are dominant. Typical examples include flexible transmission devices,

tensioned strings or cables, vibration structures, acoustic channels, and pipe-like transmis-

sion mechanisms where force, displacement, pressure, or velocity waves carry the control

action through space [5], [6]. Compared with diffusion models, a wave equation is hyper-

bolic and essentially conservative unless damping is introduced through the boundary or the

domain. Therefore, the location and form of the boundary conditions are not only modeling

details; they determine how energy enters, leaves, and persists in the actuator dynamics.

      The system considered in this thesis has precisely this PDE–ODE cascade structure. The

boundary input U (t) is applied at the right endpoint x = D of a one-dimensional wave actu-

ator u(x, t), while the left endpoint x = 0 is equipped with a positive Robin elastic support

ux (0, t) = q u(0, t), q > 0. The finite-dimensional plant state X(t) is then driven through

the distributed wave field, not through a direct scalar input. More specifically, the plant

receives the in-domain effects of both the displacement and velocity components through
           RD                      RD
the terms 0 B1 (x)u(x, t) dx and 0 B2 (x)ut (x, t) dx. Such a model captures situations

in which the plant is coupled to the whole actuator profile rather than to one measured or

actuated boundary trace.

      This distributed coupling makes the stabilization problem different from both a pure

delay problem and a single-boundary interconnection. A delay model retains only the past

values of a lumped input, while a boundary-trace model transmits information through one

endpoint variable. Here, the ODE subsystem is affected by the spatially integrated wave

displacement and wave velocity, so the closed-loop system is not in a simple strict-feedback

form. A controller that reconstructs the whole wave state can therefore become difficult

to implement. The objective of this thesis is to show that, under suitable low-gain spectral

conditions, a boundary feedback law can still stabilize the coupled system by combining

finite-dimensional low-gain damping with a controller-injected boundary velocity damping

1.2    Related Work
      In past decades, boundary control of wave equations has been extensively studied, see,

for example, [7], [8]. A recent survey by Vazquez et al. gives a broad account of PDE

backstepping developments and applications [9]. For actuator dynamics governed by a wave

PDE, an explicit compensator was first constructed in [10] for the case where the wave enters

the plant through a single point of its spatial domain. The results were later extended in [11] to

the distributed-interconnection case, where the wave PDE enters the ODE through in-domain

coupling terms rather than through a single boundary trace. As also observed through the

distributed-delay and diffusion analogues [12], [13], the key difficulty is that the overall sys-

tem, consisting of the finite-dimensional state and the infinite-dimensional actuator states, is

no longer in strict-feedback form. In addition, the wave subsystem is second-order in time,

couples displacement and velocity states, and has infinitely many imaginary-axis modes due


                                                              2
to finite propagation speed. It is therefore noted from [10], [11] that backstepping-based con-

trollers for wave actuator dynamics, especially in the distributed-interconnection case, tend

to be algebraically involved and typically require full distributed-state information, which

makes implementation difficult in practice.

     Recent studies have further moved wave and hyperbolic PDE–ODE cascades toward

output feedback, disturbances, and delays: Than and Wang treated observation delay in

an ODE–wave cascade [14]; Mei used an infinite-dimensional extended state observer for

a control-matched disturbance [15]; Feng and Zhang combined ADRC, observer design,

and backstepping for a disturbed wave–ODE cascade [16]; Wang and Diagne developed

delay-adaptive boundary control for hyperbolic PDE–ODE cascades with unknown input

delay [17]. These results show the increasing capability of wave and hyperbolic cascade

control, but they also illustrate that observer, disturbance-estimator, backstepping, or adap-

tive modules are often needed in more general information or uncertainty settings.

     On the other hand, low-gain feedback has also been a very successful approach to con-

trol design for linear systems with various input delays [18], [19]. Low-gain feedback was

first proposed in [20] to establish semiglobal stabilizability of linear systems subject to input

saturation. It was then widely applied to systems with various input nonlinearities and/or

input delays [18], [19], [21], [22]. More recently, low-gain feedback has been further ap-

plied to stabilization problems of linear systems with unbounded input delays [23]-[25]. It is

noted from these existing works [3], [18], [19], [26] that, for stabilization problems of linear

systems with input delays, low-gain feedback can provide much more simplified controllers

than predictor feedback under additional assumptions on the open-loop plant.

     Closer to the distributed-effect mechanism considered here, Xu et al. established low-

gain compensation for diffusion-counter-convection PDE–ODE cascades with distributed

effects [27]; their later backstepping-forwarding design for doubly-distributed delay–PDE–

ODE systems shows how distributed interconnections increase controller and proof com-

plexity [28]. These developments motivate asking whether a similar low-gain simplification

can be obtained for a conservative wave actuator with distributed displacement and velocity

coupling.


                                               3
1.3    Motivation
      The observations above motivate this thesis to consider low-gain feedback compensa-

tion for systems with input dynamics modeled by distributed wave PDEs. This thesis pro-

poses a low-gain controller for linear systems with distributed input dynamics governed by

a wave equation, which can be formulated as a wave PDE–ODE cascade with distributed

displacement and velocity interconnections. It is shown that the concerned system can be

exponentially stabilized with the proposed low-gain controller as long as the open-loop plant

is not exponentially unstable. It is also shown that the proposed low-gain controller has a

much more simplified form than those developed via the backstepping approach in [10], [11].

However, at the same time, additional restrictions on the eigenvalues of the open-loop plant

have to be imposed for the low-gain controller, which are not required in the backstepping

design. In contrast to the diffusion setting, the wave subsystem is conservative and does not

contain intrinsic dissipation. For this reason, the present design explicitly exploits the posi-

tive Robin elastic support at the unactuated boundary and the controller-injected damping at

the actuated boundary in order to inject decay into the infinite-dimensional system.

1.4    Thesis Work and Organization
      Main contributions of this work, compared with relevant existing works, can be sum-

marized as follows.


   1. To the best of the author’s knowledge, this thesis is the first effort on utilizing the

       low-gain feedback approach for boundary control problems with wave PDE actuator

       dynamics. The low-gain feedback approach has been successful in control problems

       of linear systems with input saturations and/or input delays [18]-[20]. More recently, it

       has been extended to diffusion PDE–ODE cascades in [27]. However, it has not been

       applied to distributed wave PDE–ODE cascades yet. This work reveals a new area

       where low-gain feedback can be applied.

   2. Compared with the backstepping-designed controllers in [10], [11] for linear systems

       with wave actuator dynamics, the controller developed in this thesis has a more sim-

       plified form and is thus easier to implement because full information of the distributed

                                               4
      actuator is not required in the feedback law. The proposed design uses only the finite-

      dimensional ODE state X(t) and the local boundary velocity ut (D, t) in the online

      feedback law, while the effective control gain is determined through a kernel boundary-

      value problem. However, at the same time, additional restrictions on the eigenvalues

      of the open-loop plant have to be imposed for the low-gain controller, whereas they

      are not required in the backstepping design.

   3. This thesis considers a distributed wave PDE–ODE cascade equipped with a posi-

      tive Robin elastic support ux (0, t) = q u(0, t), q > 0, at the unactuated boundary

      and controller-injected boundary damping at the actuated boundary. This is a posi-

      tive Robin boundary condition rather than the most general Robin class; the positivity

      of q is essential for the coercive wave energy containing the endpoint term 2q u2 (0, t).

      Unlike the diffusion case, where decay is inherited from the parabolic operator, the

      present wave subsystem is conservative and requires a dedicated energy construction

      to capture the boundary elastic energy, the controller-injected damping term, and the

      distributed coupling through both displacement and velocity channels. This thesis in-

      troduces a wave-energy Lyapunov functional with suitable cross terms and rigorously

      establishes exponential stability in a natural energy norm under the low-gain spectral

      conditions.


     The remainder of this thesis is organized as follows. Section 2 recalls the low-gain

algebraic Riccati framework and the technical inequalities used later. Section 3 develops

the PDE–ODE model, the kernel transformation, the controller, the stability proof, and the

nilpotent special case. Section 3.4 reports numerical examples. Section 4 concludes the

thesis and discusses future work.

Assumption 1. The matrix A has all its eigenvalues on the imaginary axis, the matrix E in

(23) is invertible, and the matrix pair (A, B) is controllable.

      Assumption 1 is the principal restriction introduced by the low-gain design. It does

not imply stability or boundedness of the open-loop cascade, but it covers important classes

of systems such as oscillators and nilpotent integrator chains. By Lemma 1, applied with

B = B, the algebraic Riccati equation


                               AT P + P A − P BBT P = −εP.                             (35)


has a unique positive definite solution P = Pε . The proof below will restrict ε > 0 to be

sufficiently small and use the low-gain scaling properties stated in Lemma 1. The proposed

boundary controller is

                               U (t) = −BT P X(t) − b ut (D, t).                       (36)

Remark 1. The function g(x) and the vector B are computed offline. Once B is obtained,

the online controller (36) only uses X(t) and ut (D, t).

Remark 2. The term −BT P X(t) is the low-gain stabilizing part for the ODE channel, while

−b ut (D, t) injects boundary damping into the wave equation. Hence the controller has a

simpler online form than a backstepping controller requiring full distributed-state informa-

tion, although this simplification comes with the additional spectral restriction in Assump-

tion 1.

3.5    Chapter Summary
      This chapter has presented the main low-gain boundary stabilization result for the wave

PDE–ODE cascade. It first stated the system model and energy objective, then gave the

offline gain computation, the low-gain Riccati equation, the boundary controller, and the

stability proof. In the proof, the Riccati term provides finite-dimensional dissipation, while

the controller-injected boundary damping absorbs the remaining wave terms for sufficiently

small ε. The nilpotent case further illustrates that the general construction reduces to a sim-

pler explicit endpoint gain when the ODE dynamics have all eigenvalues at the origin.




                                                           37
                       4.    Conclusion and Future Work
4.1    Summary
      This thesis studied the low-gain stabilization problem for a class of wave PDE–ODE

cascade systems with distributed in-domain coupling, a positive Robin elastic support at the

unactuated boundary, and controller-injected boundary damping at the actuated boundary.

By a suitable transformation, the distributed input dynamics reduce to a low-gain design

problem with an explicit effective input vector B. For the general case, the controller was

constructed as U (t) = −B⊤ Pε X(t) − b ut (D, t), where Pε is the low-gain Riccati solution.

For the nilpotent case, the transformation and the effective gain were further simplified,

which yielded a more transparent controller formula.

      The main theoretical result shows that, under the low-gain spectral assumption on the

ODE dynamics and the boundary damping injected by the controller, the closed-loop system

is exponentially stable in a natural energy norm. The proof relies on a Lyapunov functional

that combines the Riccati-based ODE term with the damped wave energy and suitable cross

terms, thereby capturing the effect of the distributed displacement and velocity couplings. In

comparison with existing backstepping controllers for wave actuator dynamics, the present

design has a much simpler feedback structure because it uses only the finite-dimensional state

X(t) and the local boundary velocity ut (D, t) online. The price for this simplification is that

stronger assumptions on the open-loop ODE dynamics are required than in the backstepping

framework.

      The numerical examples confirm the theoretical conclusions for both the general and

nilpotent settings, showing clear decay of the ODE states and the distributed wave profile un-

der the proposed controller. Overall, this work extends the low-gain compensation method-

ology from diffusion PDE–ODE cascades to the wave-equation setting and shows that a

boundary damping term in the controller can provide the dissipation needed for the low-gain

mechanism to succeed in a hyperbolic system.
